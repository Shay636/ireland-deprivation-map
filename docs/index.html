Ireland, 2006 to 2022

Two Decades of Disadvantage

Addiction Treatment Access and Deprivation in Ireland

728,000 people in Ireland have lived in persistently deprived areas for at least twenty years. These are places that fell into the bottom quartile of the national deprivation index in 2006 and have never left it. Many of the same areas are also the furthest from addiction treatment services. This map traces that overlap across four census waves: use the time slider to watch how deprivation has shifted, held, or deepened since the Celtic Tiger era. Proximity to a service is not the same as access, but it is where access starts.

Key findings

728,000 people living in areas that were deprived in every census wave from 2006 to 2022

Across the country, 434 Electoral Divisions have sat in the most deprived quartile at every census since 2006. These are not temporary pockets of hardship. They are places the recovery of the Celtic Tiger era, and the post-crash stabilisation that followed, largely passed by.

Donegal stands out most starkly in rural Ireland: 79 persistently deprived Electoral Divisions, representing around 85,000 people, spread across a county where the average Electoral Division is 24.6 km from the nearest listed treatment service. Distance here compounds every other disadvantage.

The single most isolated area in the country is Doonloughan in Connemara, County Galway, which is 72.7 km in a straight line from the nearest listed addiction treatment service. Nine of the ten most isolated Electoral Divisions are in either west Galway or Kerry, all more than 61 km from help. The distance layer on the map above shows this pattern across every part of the country.

Dublin City contains the largest population in persistently deprived areas: roughly 113,000 people across 38 Electoral Divisions, concentrated in the north and south inner city. Yet Dublin City records zero underserved areas on the access metric, because services are densely distributed across the capital. That apparent contradiction reflects a real tension. Services exist, but so do waiting lists, referral barriers, and the everyday difficulty of reaching help when your life is in crisis. The same pattern applies to Cork City, Limerick City, and Waterford City. Where they exist, urban services cover the ground. They do not always cover the need.

Limitations

  • Service locations reflect December 2025 only. No systematic historical record of service locations across the four census years was available. Services have opened, moved, and closed; the 2025 data is used as a fixed reference layer and should not be read as a measure of access at any earlier point.
  • GP-based treatment is excluded. Medication-assisted treatment (methadone, buprenorphine) prescribed through general practitioners is not represented in the service locations shown. In areas with active GP prescribers this may substantially understate real access, particularly in rural settings where community pharmacies hold dispensing contracts.
  • Distance is a proxy, not a measure of access. The analysis uses straight-line distance to the nearest listed service. It does not account for public transport availability, waiting times, service capacity, operating hours, or any of the social and structural barriers that determine whether someone can actually engage with treatment.
  • The 2022 deprivation scores use the official Pobal HP Index at Electoral Division level. A companion small-area analysis derives scores from CSO SAPS 2022 microdata at finer geographic resolution. Both use the same underlying census data; the Pobal index is used here to keep the methodology consistent across all four historical waves.
  • 3.2% of historical Electoral Divisions are unmatched. Electoral Division boundaries changed between the 2006 and 2022 censuses. Divisions that were merged, split, or renamed cannot be reliably linked and appear grey in the 2006, 2011, and 2016 layers. The 2022 layer is fully matched.
